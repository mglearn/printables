Analyzing an Argument, Evidence, and Revision

Grade: 8 Subject: Reading / Language Arts Time: ~45 min core + ~15 min extension RLA

Overview

This is a self-contained, no-technology substitute packet in which students read an original persuasive passage, "Later Bells," and analyze its argument. Working alone with a pencil, students predict the author's position, read the passage, identify the claim, judge which support is strong and which is weak, spot a statement that is unsupported or irrelevant, revise a weak point using real evidence from the passage, and choose the evidence that best supports the claim. They finish with a short response stating a revised claim backed by two pieces of evidence, judging their quality. Every text-dependent question can be answered directly from the printed passage.

Start (5 minutes) — Notice & Predict

  1. The passage is titled "Later Bells: Why Our School Day Should Start at 9:00." From the title alone, predict the author's position — what does the writer want to happen, and what side of the issue are they on?
  2. Retrieval: skim paragraph 1. According to the author, what time does the school day currently begin?

Build (10 minutes) — Read the argument

Words to know (for analyzing an argument)

claim
the main point or position the writer is trying to prove.
evidence
facts, data, examples, or expert statements used to back up a claim.
reasoning
the thinking that explains how the evidence supports the claim.
counterargument
a point on the other side that an opponent might make.
relevance
how directly a piece of evidence connects to the claim; whether it truly matters here.

"Later Bells: Why Our School Day Should Start at 9:00" — an original argument

Paragraph 1. Our school day begins at 7:45 in the morning, which means most students set an alarm before the sun is even up. I believe Franklin Middle School should move its start time to 9:00. A later start would leave students healthier, more alert, and better prepared to learn. This is not about laziness; it is about matching the schedule to how eighth-grade bodies actually work.

Paragraph 2. The strongest reason is biological. According to a 2014 report from the American Academy of Pediatrics, the natural sleep rhythms of teenagers shift later during adolescence, so most teens cannot fall asleep before about 11:00 p.m. The report recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. so students can get the 8 to 10 hours of sleep their growing brains need. When the science of teenage sleep points one direction, our bell schedule should not point the other.

Paragraph 3. There is also evidence that later start times help students do better in school. In a study of several districts that pushed their start times later, researchers found that attendance improved and the number of students who were late to first period dropped. Students who sleep enough are simply more able to focus, remember, and participate. A rested student is a ready student.

Paragraph 4. Some people say a later start is impossible because of the bus schedule. But buses are a solvable problem, not a reason to keep tired students in early classes. Districts across the country have redesigned bus routes to fit later bells, and if they can do it, so can we.

Paragraph 5. Besides, mornings are just unpleasant. Nobody I know enjoys waking up early, and my cousin, who is in college, says her classes never start before ten. The cafeteria also serves a better breakfast on the days it offers pancakes, which proves mornings could be improved in many ways.

Paragraph 6. Moving the first bell to 9:00 is a change that fits the science, supports learning, and treats students like the growing young people they are. The alarm clock should work with our brains, not against them. It is time for later bells.

  1. In paragraph 2, what does the author say happens to teenagers' sleep rhythms during adolescence? Answer using details from the passage.

Apply (20 minutes) — Analyze the argument

  1. Identify the claim: in one sentence, state the author's main claim — the position the whole passage is trying to prove. (paragraph 1)
  2. Evaluate evidence quality: paragraph 2 and paragraph 5 each try to support the claim. One uses strong evidence and one uses weak evidence. Decide which is which and explain why (is the support factual, relevant, and reliable?).
  3. Spot the irrelevant support: paragraph 5 contains a sentence that does not actually help prove the claim about start times. Identify that sentence and explain why it is irrelevant.
  4. Revision task: paragraph 5 uses weak reasons. Rewrite that support so it is stronger — replace one weak reason with real evidence from the passage (paragraph 2 or 3) that genuinely supports the 9:00 start. Write your improved sentence.
  5. Which evidence best supports the claim (that the day should start at 9:00)? Choose one.
    • A. "The cafeteria also serves a better breakfast on the days it offers pancakes."
    • B. "My cousin, who is in college, says her classes never start before ten."
    • C. "The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m."
    • D. "Nobody I know enjoys waking up early."
    Then (8b) explain in one sentence why the evidence you chose is stronger than the others.

Explain (5–10 minutes) — State a revised claim & judge your evidence

Question 9. Write a revised, focused claim about the school start time (you may agree with the author, disagree, or narrow the claim — for example, "Franklin Middle School should start at 8:30 because that fits the pediatricians' recommendation"). Support it with two pieces of evidence from the passage (with paragraph numbers), and for each one judge its quality — explain why it is strong.

Sentence stems you may use: "My revised claim is…"; "One strong piece of evidence is… (paragraph ___).": "A second piece of evidence is… (paragraph ___).": "This evidence is strong because it is factual / from an expert / directly relevant…"

Close (5 minutes) — ACE

  • Articulate: say the author's claim in your own words, in one sentence.
  • Connect: point to one piece of strong evidence in the passage (name the paragraph) that best backs up that claim.
  • Extend: name one weak spot in the argument and tell what the author could add to make it stronger.

Continue (optional, ~15 minutes) — Early finisher

Write a counterargument: one paragraph from the opposite side arguing the school day should not move to 9:00, with at least one reason (for example, after-school jobs, sports, or working parents). Then write one sentence explaining how the author of "Later Bells" could respond to that counterargument.

Turn in

Hand in the whole packet with your name, class period, and date, with questions 1–9 and the ACE box answered. Include the optional counterargument if you did it.
