Principles of the U.S. Constitution

Grade: 8 Subject: Social Studies (U.S. History to Reconstruction) Time: ~45 min core + ~15 min extension Social Studies

Overview

This is a self-contained, no-technology substitute packet in which students explore the five core principles of the U.S. Constitution — popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism. Working alone with a pencil, students read two short original summaries, study a diagram of the three branches, do source analysis on a brief public-domain line from the Preamble (creator, date, type), match each principle to an example, distinguish fact from interpretation and opinion, and write an evidence-based explanation of which principle a scenario best illustrates. Prompts are non-partisan and kept in historical context.

Start (5 minutes) — Observe a source

Public-domain excerpt — the opening of the Preamble to the U.S. Constitution (1787): "We the People of the United States, in Order to form a more perfect Union … do ordain and establish this Constitution for the United States of America." (Type: primary source, public domain; creator: the framers at the Constitutional Convention; date: 1787; only the first and last phrases are shown.)

  1. Write one thing you notice about who the excerpt says the government comes from, and predict one idea the framers thought was important based on the words "We the People."
  2. Why might the framers have wanted to divide the government's power among different groups instead of giving it all to one person? Give one reason in your own words.

Build (5–10 minutes) — Read the sources

Source A — The five core principles (written for this packet)

When the framers wrote the U.S. Constitution in 1787, they built it on a small set of guiding ideas. Popular sovereignty means the government's power comes from the people; voters choose leaders, who answer to the public, and the Preamble begins "We the People." Limited government means the government may only do what the Constitution allows, and officials are not above the law. Separation of powers means the work of government is split among three branches: the legislative branch (Congress) makes laws, the executive branch (the President) carries out laws, and the judicial branch (the courts) interprets laws. Checks and balances means each branch can limit the other two so no branch grows too strong — for example, Congress passes a bill, the President can veto it, and Congress can override the veto with a large enough vote. Federalism means power is shared between the national (federal) government and the state governments: some powers belong to the nation, some to the states, and some are shared. (Type: original explanatory summary; focus: U.S. constitutional principles, 1787.)

Source B — A design note (written for this packet)

This is an original summary, not a real quotation. It restates a common idea historians use to describe the framers' goal: "The framers had lived under a distant king and had also seen a national government too weak to work. They wanted a middle path: a government strong enough to act, but tied down by written rules so that no one person or single branch could seize control." (Type: original summary of a historical viewpoint; creator: not a real person; focus: the framers' intent, about 1787; not a genuine primary quotation.)

Word bank

  1. Using the diagram, write the job of each branch in a few words: Legislative, Executive, Judicial.

Apply (20–25 minutes) — Analyze the sources

  1. Match each principle to the best example (each used once): popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism. Examples: (A) the President can veto a bill and Congress can override the veto; (B) voters elect the members of Congress; (C) some powers belong to the nation and some to the states; (D) officials must follow the written rules of the Constitution; (E) Congress makes laws, the President carries them out, the courts interpret them.
  2. Source analysis of the Preamble excerpt from Question 1: identify the creator, the date, and the type of source (primary or secondary? public domain?), then name one principle from Source A that the words "We the People" help show and explain in one sentence.
  3. Why did the framers include checks and balances? In 2–3 sentences, using Source B and the diagram, explain what problem they were trying to prevent and how one branch checking another helps.
  4. Fact (F), interpretation (I), or opinion (O)? Mark each:
    • a) The Constitution divides the national government into three branches.
    • b) The framers probably included checks and balances because they feared one branch gaining too much power.
    • c) The judicial branch is the most important branch of all.
    • d) Under federalism, power is shared between the national government and the states.
  5. Evaluate a claim: a student claims "Separation of powers and federalism are just two names for the same thing." Do the sources support it? Choose: (A) Yes — both mean splitting power among the three branches; (B) No — separation of powers divides power among three branches, while federalism divides power between the nation and the states; (C) The sources never mention federalism. Then explain your choice with one detail from Source A or the diagram.

Explain (5–10 minutes) — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Question 9. Scenario (written for this packet): "Congress passes a new law. The President disagrees and refuses to sign it, sending it back. Congress then votes again, and this time enough members vote for it that it becomes law anyway." Which single principle does this scenario best illustrate, and how does the evidence support that? Write a claim that names one principle, give two pieces of evidence from the scenario, Source A, or the diagram, then explain your reasoning.

Sentence stems you may use: "This scenario best illustrates ______ because…"; "One piece of evidence is… (from the scenario / Source A / the diagram)."; "A second piece of evidence is…"; "This shows the principle because…"

Close (5 minutes) — ACE

  • Articulate: explain in your own words what checks and balances means.
  • Connect: point to one arrow or box in the diagram, or one line in a source, that shows one branch limiting another.
  • Extend: name one everyday group where power is divided so no one person decides everything, and give one reason that helps.

Continue (optional, ~15 minutes) — Early finisher

Remove a check and predict: imagine the Constitution had no way for Congress to override a presidential veto. Using only the sources and the diagram, predict two ways the balance of power might change. For each, name the check you removed and the effect you expect on how the branches work together.

Turn in

Hand in the whole packet with your name, class period, and date, with questions 1–9 and the ACE box answered. Include the optional prediction if you did it.
