Theme and Author's Craft in Fiction

Course: English II (Grades 9–12) Subject: English Language Arts & Reading Time: ~50-min period or ~90-min block RLA

Overview

This is a self-contained, no-technology substitute packet in which students read an original short story, "The Lantern Keeper," and analyze how the author builds meaning. Working alone with a pencil, students predict from the title, warm up on craft terms, read the story, trace how the theme develops, analyze the main character's change with text evidence, identify the author's craft element (symbolism — the lantern/light) and its effect, and choose the line that is the best evidence for the theme. They finish with a short analytical response (Claim = theme, Evidence = text, Reasoning). On a block period, they add a paragraph analyzing how the symbol shapes the theme. Every text-dependent question can be answered directly from the printed story.

Start (5 minutes) — Predict & warm up

  1. The story is titled "The Lantern Keeper." From the title alone, predict what object will matter most and what job or role the main character might have.
  2. Term warm-up. Match each craft term to its meaning: A. theme (a message about life), B. symbolism (an object standing for a bigger idea), C. characterization (how an author shows what a character is like). Which term is each of these?
    • A lit lamp in a window comes to stand for hope and purpose. (symbolism)
    • We learn a character is loyal because she keeps a promise through storms and fevers. (characterization)
    • The idea that we must let go of the past to keep serving the living. (theme)

Build (12 minutes) — Read the story

Words to know (for theme and author's craft)

theme
the deeper message or insight about life that a story reveals — not the topic, but what the story says about the topic.
characterization
the methods an author uses to show what a character is like — through words, actions, thoughts, and change.
symbolism
when a concrete object, place, or action stands for a larger idea beyond itself.
tone
the author's attitude toward the subject, created by word choice and detail.
motif
an image, idea, or detail that repeats through a story and helps build its theme.

"The Lantern Keeper" — an original short story

Paragraph 1. For eleven years, Nadia had lit the lamp in the tower window every night at dusk, exactly as her grandmother had taught her. The lighthouse had gone dark decades ago — the harbor built a taller beacon of steel and electricity down the coast — but Nadia's small lamp still burned in the old stone tower each evening. Her neighbors called it a habit. Nadia called it a promise.

Paragraph 2. The lamp was her grandmother's last request. On the night before she died, the old woman had pressed the brass lantern into Nadia's hands and whispered, "Someone out there is always looking for a light. Don't let it go out." So Nadia hadn't. Through storms, through fevers, through the long winter her mother left, the small flame had never once failed to appear in the window.

Paragraph 3. But this autumn, the fishing boats stopped coming. The harbor emptied. The village grew quiet, and Nadia began to wonder whether anyone was looking for her light at all. Each dusk she climbed the ninety-seven steps feeling heavier, the brass lantern colder in her palm, the flame smaller against the enormous dark.

Paragraph 4. One grey evening, a girl appeared at the base of the tower — soaked, shivering, no older than ten. "I saw the light from the cliff road," she said. "I was lost. I followed it down." Nadia stared at her, then at the lantern, then at the black water beyond the glass.

Paragraph 5. "I almost didn't light it tonight," Nadia admitted.

Paragraph 6. The girl frowned, as if this were the strangest thing she had ever heard. "But it's the only light out here," she said simply, and warmed her hands against the glass.

Paragraph 7. That night, after the girl had gone home safe, Nadia sat a long time with the lantern. She had believed the light was a duty owed to the dead — a debt she paid alone, in the cold, to a grandmother who could no longer see it. But the girl on the cliff road had not needed Nadia's grief. She had needed only the flame.

Paragraph 8. In the morning, Nadia carried the brass lantern down the ninety-seven steps and set it in the window of her own small kitchen, where the cliff road passed close and any traveler could see it. She lit a second lamp for the tower. The promise, she understood now, had never been about the stone tower or the darkened harbor. It had been about keeping something alight for whoever came next — and letting the rest of them go.

Paragraph 9. The lighthouse still stood dark and useless on its rock. But every evening two lights burned above the water now, and Nadia climbed the ninety-seven steps with the brass lantern feeling, for the first time in eleven years, strangely light.

  1. In paragraph 2, what exactly did Nadia's grandmother ask her to do, and in her own words, what did she say? Answer using details from the story.

Apply (20 minutes) — Analyze the story

  1. Trace how the theme develops: write what the light means to Nadia at the beginning (paragraphs 1–3), what the girl helps her see at the turning point (paragraphs 4–7), and then state the theme in one sentence (what the story says about life).
  2. Analyze characterization with evidence: Nadia is a dynamic character (she changes). Copy one detail from early in the story that shows how she first sees her task, and one from late in the story that shows how she has changed; name the paragraph for each, then explain the change.
  3. Identify the craft element and its effect: the author uses symbolism — the lantern/light stands for more than a flame. What does the light come to symbolize by the end, and how does that symbol help the reader understand the theme?
  4. Which line is the best evidence for the theme (that we must let go of the past to keep serving the living)? Choose one.
    • A. "The lighthouse had gone dark decades ago."
    • B. "It had been about keeping something alight for whoever came next — and letting the rest of them go."
    • C. "Each dusk she climbed the ninety-seven steps feeling heavier."
    • D. "I saw the light from the cliff road."
    Then (7b) explain in one sentence why the line you chose supports the theme better than the others.

Explain (10–15 minutes) — Analytical response on theme

Question 8. Write an analytical response (not a plot summary) that states the story's theme and proves it from the text. Use the Claim–Evidence–Reasoning frame: your Claim is the theme, your Evidence is at least two details or quotations from the story (name the paragraph for each), and your Reasoning explains how that evidence develops the theme.

Sentence stems you may use: "The theme of 'The Lantern Keeper' is that…"; "One detail that develops this theme is ___ in paragraph ___, which shows…"; "The author also shows this when ___ (paragraph ___); this matters because…"; "Together, these details reveal that…"

Question 9 (block period only). Add a paragraph analyzing how the author's symbolism — the lantern and light — shapes the theme. Explain what the light means at the start, what it means at the end, and how that shift in the symbol makes the theme land more powerfully than a stated message would. Cite at least one paragraph.

Close (5 minutes) — ACE

  • Articulate: say the story's theme in your own words, in one sentence.
  • Connect: name the one moment where Nadia changes the most, and point to the paragraph.
  • Extend: the last line calls Nadia "strangely light" — explain the double meaning of the word light there and why the author ends on it.

Continue (optional) — Early finisher

Write the girl's version: retell the night she followed the light down the cliff road from the girl's point of view. Keep the same theme, but use at least one symbol of your own (an object that stands for a bigger idea) and label which detail is your symbol.

Turn in

Hand in the whole packet with your name, class period, and date, with questions 1–8 and the ACE box answered. Question 9 is for block periods; include the optional rewrite if you did it.
