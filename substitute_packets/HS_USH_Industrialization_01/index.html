Industrialization and Its Effects

Course: U.S. History Studies Since 1877 Grades: 9–12 Time: ~50 min standard · ~90 min block Social Studies

Overview

This is a self-contained, no-technology substitute packet in which high-school students investigate American industrialization (roughly the 1870s–1910s): its causes, its effects on workers, cities, and immigration, and the responses of labor unions and reformers. Working alone with a pencil, students read short original summaries, study a timeline of key events, do source analysis on a brief public-domain excerpt (creator, date, type, point of view), build a cause-and-effect chain, compare two sources for corroboration or contradiction, distinguish fact from interpretation and opinion, evaluate a claim, and write an evidence-based historical argument about which effect was most significant. The packet supports a ~50-minute standard version and a ~90-minute block version (the block adds a data source and extended writing). Prompts are non-partisan and grounded in historical evidence.

Accessible version of the student activity

The full student activity is reproduced below in plain, screen-reader-friendly HTML. It reflows on phones and at 200% zoom. Write your answers on the printed packet. Items marked [BLOCK] are for the ~90-minute version only.

Start (5 minutes) — Observe a source

Public-domain excerpt — from the preamble to the U.S. federal law creating the Department of Commerce and Labor (1903): "…to foster, promote, and develop the foreign and domestic commerce, the mining, manufacturing, shipping, and fishery industries, [and] the labor interests… of the United States." (Type: primary source, public domain, an Act of Congress; creator: the U.S. Congress; date: 1903; only a portion is shown.)

  1. Write one thing you notice about which parts of the economy the government said it wanted to develop by 1903, and predict one way daily life or work in America might have been changing.
  2. Why might building large factories in cities change where and how ordinary people lived and worked? Give one reason in your own words.

Build (10–15 minutes) — Read the sources

Source A — What drove and defined industrialization (written for this packet)

Between the 1870s and the 1910s, the United States changed from a mostly farming nation into the world's leading industrial power. Causes: vast natural resources (coal, iron ore, oil, timber, water power); a national network of railroads that moved raw materials to factories and goods to markets; new technology and machines (such as the Bessemer process for cheap steel) that raised output; a growing supply of workers, including farm migrants and millions of immigrants; and investment capital to build mills and mines. Effects on work: production moved into large factories organized by the division of labor, which raised output but often meant long hours, low pay, monotonous work, and unsafe conditions. Effects on cities and immigration: factories clustered in cities, so people moved toward the jobs; cities grew rapidly (urbanization) and many immigrants arrived to fill industrial jobs. Responses: workers formed labor unions to bargain for better wages, shorter hours, and safer conditions, and over time reformers and governments passed new laws (for example, limits on child labor and safety rules), though change came unevenly and slowly. (Type: original explanatory summary; focus: causes and effects of U.S. industrialization, about 1870s–1910s.)

Source B — A factory-floor description (written for this packet)

This is an original summary, not a real quotation. It restates conditions historians commonly describe: "In many mills the workday ran ten to twelve hours, six days a week. Machines set the pace, and a worker who fell behind could be replaced the same afternoon. The air was thick with dust and noise; injuries were common and rarely paid for. Wages were low enough that whole families — including children — often worked to make ends meet." (Type: original summary of typical conditions; creator: not a real person; focus: industrial working conditions, about 1870s–1910s; not a genuine primary quotation.)

Source C — Urban growth data [BLOCK]

An original data summary (illustrative, rounded) reflecting the documented rise in the urban share of the U.S. population as factories grew: in 1870 about 1 in 4 Americans (≈25%) lived in cities, in a mostly rural nation; in 1890 about 1 in 3 (≈35%), with cities growing fast; in 1910 about 4 in 9 (≈45%), nearly half urban. (Type: original data summary; focus: U.S. urbanization, 1870–1910; treat exact percents as approximate.)

Word bank

industrialization
the shift from making goods by hand to making them by machine in factories.
urbanization
the growth of cities as more people move to and live in them.
immigration
people moving into a country to live and work.
division of labor
splitting production into small tasks, each done by different workers.
capital
money invested to build or run a business, such as factories or machines.
labor union
an organized group of workers who bargain together for better pay and conditions.
reform
a change meant to correct a problem, often through new laws or rules.
cause
a reason something happens; it comes before an effect.
effect
a result or consequence of a cause.
point of view
the perspective from which a source was created.
fact
a statement that can be checked and shown true or false.
interpretation
an explanation of what evidence means; reasoned from facts.
opinion
a personal belief or preference; not proven by evidence.
  1. Using the timeline, list the two earliest events and the two latest events with their dates.

Apply (20–25 minutes) — Analyze the sources

  1. Source analysis of the 1903 excerpt from Question 1: identify the creator, the date, and the type of source (primary or secondary? public domain?), then describe its point of view — who created it and how that might shape what it emphasizes.
  2. Cause-and-effect chain: using Source A, name two causes and two effects so the chain reads from cause → industrialization (factories mass-produce goods) → effects.
  3. Trace one complete chain in 2–3 sentences: pick a cause from Source A, explain how it helped industrialization grow, and name one effect on workers, cities, or immigration.
  4. Corroborate or contradict: compare Source A (overview) and Source B (factory-floor description). Do they mostly corroborate or contradict about working conditions? Explain with one detail from each source.
  5. Fact (F), interpretation (I), or opinion (O)? Mark each:
    • a) Railroads helped move raw materials to factories and goods to markets.
    • b) Long hours and unsafe conditions probably pushed many workers to form unions.
    • c) Factory work in this era was the worst kind of job anyone could ever have.
    • d) By 1910, close to half of Americans lived in cities.
  6. Evaluate a claim: a student claims "Industrialization was caused only by new machines — nothing else mattered." Do the sources support it? Choose: (A) Yes — Source A says machines were the single cause; (B) No — Source A names several causes working together (resources, railroads, technology, workers, capital), not machines alone; (C) The sources never mention machines. Then explain your choice with one detail from Source A.
  7. [BLOCK] Using Source C, describe the trend in the urban share of the U.S. population from 1870 to 1910, then explain in one sentence how industrialization (Source A) helps explain that trend.

Explain (10–20 minutes) — Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Question 11. Of industrialization's effects on labor, cities, and immigration, which do you think was the most significant for the United States in this period? Write a claim that names one effect, support it with two pieces of evidence from the sources or timeline, then explain your reasoning. There is no single "right" effect — argue from the evidence. Sentence stems: "The most significant effect of industrialization was ______ because…"; "One piece of evidence is… (from Source A / B / C or the timeline)."; "A second piece of evidence is…"; "This evidence supports my claim because…"

[BLOCK] Extended writing: bring in a second source or timeline event as added evidence, and add a counter-consideration — name one reason someone might argue a different effect was most significant, then explain why you still hold your claim or how it changes.

Close (5 minutes) — ACE

  • Articulate: explain in your own words what industrialization was.
  • Connect: point to one timeline event or source line that shows a cause of industrialization and one that shows an effect; name both.
  • Extend: name one everyday product or job today that exists because of factory-based, machine production, and give one reason it connects to this history.

Continue (optional, ~15 minutes) — Early finisher

Match a reform to a problem: pick one problem named in the sources (long hours, unsafe conditions, or child labor), then name which reform response from Source A (labor unions, or new laws such as limits on child labor and safety rules) best addressed it, and why, using one detail from the sources or timeline.

Turn in

Hand in the whole packet with your name, class period, and date, with questions 1–11 and the ACE box answered (skip [BLOCK] items if you did the standard version). Include the optional early-finisher task if you did it.
